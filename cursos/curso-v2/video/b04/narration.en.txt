ClearView already had its three qualities: being able to test it, being able to pay for it, and being able to talk to many outside systems. With them it had to choose the overall shape of the system, what is called its style. At one end is the monolith, a single piece deployed whole. At the other, microservices, many small pieces deployed separately. Between the two there are several in-between shapes, and each one is good at some qualities and bad at others. The team chose with a spreadsheet of styles put together by Mark Richards, one of the people who present the competition. It has a column per style and a row per quality, and each cell carries from one to five stars. The team marked the rows of its qualities and looked at which column added up to more where it mattered.

The service-based style won, a few large services that share the database. It has four stars in cost, which the team uses to measure whether it can pay for it, and four in testability. The team writes that it is cheaper and faster to build and easier to maintain. But in interoperability, the third one, it has only two stars. And ClearView did not let it go. Where the system has to talk to the companies' human resources systems, it uses another style, an event-driven one. In that style, the parts notify each other with messages instead of calling and waiting for the answer. In the spreadsheet, it has one more star in that row. So the overall shape is chosen by the qualities that rule, and a part with another need can step away from it. The decision also says what it loses. Sharing the database weakens elasticity. When a peak arrives, each service can add copies, but they all end up waiting on the same database.

Farmacy Food sells prepared meals that are picked up from smart fridges, and it works on a very small budget. It had the same money pressure as ClearView, and it got somewhere else. It chose a modular monolith, a single application divided inside into modules with clear borders. Its reasons come from the state of the business. The team is small, it has to launch fast and the budget is short. Besides, it writes that microservices need a stable, well-understood business, and that for its ordering system that is not true yet. Microservices, it says, should grow as they become needed. The cost is noted too. It takes discipline to keep the modules from mixing over time.

ClearView and Farmacy Food had little money, and they put it above everything. One ended up with a few large services and the other with a single application, because other things weighed in as well. In one case, the need to talk to many outside systems. In the other, a business that was still moving. Neither chose a style with no costs. Each chose the one whose costs it could pay, and left in writing what they were.
